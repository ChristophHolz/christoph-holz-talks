Man and Machine — an unbeatable team

How working alongside cognitive robots and AI gives work back its humanity.

Our great-grandparents did inhuman work in the factories of the Industrial Revolution — because there was no automation yet, no robots, no AI. That work was never made for humans. Once everything inhuman has been digitized away, what remains is what makes us us — the human being. And anyone who thinks AI or robots create unemployment simply hasn't understood what work actually is.

Three Thoughts from the Talk

It's not about replacing people, but about filling positions for which there are no people left.

When all inhuman work is digitalized away, what remains is the human being.

Anyone who thinks AI or robots are taking our jobs has obviously never done any real work themselves.

Questions and Answers about the Talk

Does the talk address the specific needs of the audience?

Yes: examples and focal points are tailored to the industry in advance with the organizer — here, for instance, to automation, the skills shortage, and robots.

Are participants met where they are?

Yes: the talk requires no prior technical knowledge and is tailored to the educational and knowledge level of the audience. Usually Holz joins in beforehand to get to know his listeners.

Is the talk interactive?

Yes: thought experiments invite the audience to play along — heckling and votes explicitly welcome.

Does anything from the talk stick — and does it inspire courage or fear?

Yes — courage, to be precise: vivid stories like the great-grandparents' factory keep the conversation going long after the evening's over. Christoph Holz is deliberately optimistic — technology as a tool that empowers people.

Christoph Holz's Expertise

Industrial robots

Ever since he built his first industrial articulated-arm robot in 1986, industrial robotics has never let go of Christoph Holz: in 2014 he saw the welding robots at Tesla in Fremont, and in 2026 their next generation at Magna Steyr on the G-Class assembly line. At ABB he explored cobots. On stage, he answers the question of why robots are leaving their cages — and why an industrial robot needs hands with five fingers when three would do.

Manufacturing Industry

Even during his time at technical secondary school (HTL), Holz worked on the production floor at Kahlbacher, the world market leader for snow-clearing technology, and at Krupp in Essen. After his combined studies in computer science and mechanical engineering, Holz consulted for BMW engine manufacturing in Lohof. In his entrepreneurial career, Holz led projects for voestalpine Stahl AG, Thyssen and EGLO. Today Magna, Egger and Siemens bring him to the stage — where he answers the question of why the future of the factory is not a factory: the plant hall solves a problem that will soon no longer exist.

TV expert

Christoph Holz appears regularly as a TV expert on digitalization and AI — including on RTL, ServusTV, ProSieben, Sat.1, n-tv, WELT, Deutsche Welle, Hamburg1, ORF and CNBC.
