Welcoming the panellists

Gregor Weihs

On my left, Professor Gregor Weihs from Innsbruck. In 1998, using 400 metres of optical fibre strung across the university campus, he helped show that Einstein was wrong about this one. Physics awarded a Nobel Prize for that line of work in 2022 — to Vienna. Professor Weihs, in Tyrol you are used to that sort of border arrangement.
From 2027 he becomes Rector of the University of Innsbruck. So he moves from a discipline where uncertainty can be measured precisely, into one where nobody has ever managed it.

Mete Atatüre

On my right, Professor Mete Atatüre, Head of the Cavendish Laboratory in Cambridge. That is the building where the electron was found, and the neutron, and the structure of DNA. You could say that whoever turns the lights off there is making scientific history.
In 2015 his group squeezed light — three per cent below what the vacuum is supposed to allow. Three per cent sounds modest, until you realise he took on nothing itself and won.

Anecdotes and detail to follow up on

The 1998 Bell test. Alice and Bob sat 400 metres apart across the Innsbruck science campus. The measurement setting was chosen by a quantum random number generator — an LED on a beam splitter, two photomultipliers behind it. Each measurement had to finish in well under 1.3 microseconds, the light travel time between the stations. Data were taken from 1997 to 1999. Published in Physical Review Letters on 7 December 1998.
The 2022 Nobel Prize. Awarded to Aspect, Clauser and Zeilinger for exactly this class of experiment. A light, non-intrusive question: "How does it feel to have built an experiment that sends somebody else to Stockholm?" Only if the room is warm.
Squeezed light, 2015. Nature 525, 222–225. An artificial atom with a large optical dipole, giving a hundredfold higher photon rate than a natural atom. The quadrature variance came in 3 % below the vacuum limit while the photon statistics stayed antibunched. The Cambridge headline at the time: "Scientists squeeze light one particle at a time."
The Cavendish. Founded 1874, more than thirty Nobel Prizes, J. J. Thomson, Rutherford, Watson and Crick. Atatüre is a Bilkent graduate from Ankara — a first for the post.
Tyrol against Cambridge. Innsbruck has around 130,000 inhabitants and a quantum density that holds its own against any capital: AQT, Parity, the Zeilinger school. A good way into question 9 on national programmes.
