The myth of artificial intelligence

Where it comes from, where it leads, what it can do — and what it will never be able to do.

Artificial intelligence is older than the hype suggests: Mary Shelley wrote it in Frankenstein in 1816, Ada Lovelace dissected it a hundred years before Alan Turing. Today no computer scientist can explain how GPT works — and yet AI claims once again to know what's true. Christoph Holz traces the arc from the steam engine to the quantum computer, from expert systems to hallucination, from European invention to American market conquest. A reality check on what the machine can do — and what it will never be able to do.

Three Thoughts from the Talk

The greatest danger of AI is not that it becomes too good. The greatest danger is our uncritical trust.

AI is not a technical term, but a philosophical creation myth. It represents computer science's eternal longing to create a life form of its own.

Anyone who believes that neural AI sometimes hallucinates is wrong. It always hallucinates. But with a little luck, it sometimes happens to agree with reality.

Questions and Answers about the Talk

Does the talk address the specific needs of the audience?

Yes: examples and focal points are tailored to your industry in advance with the organizer — here, for instance, to expert systems, hallucination and quantum computers.

Are participants met where they are?

Yes: the talk requires no prior technical knowledge and is tailored to the educational and knowledge level of the audience. Usually Holz joins in beforehand to get to know his listeners.

Is the talk interactive?

Yes: thought experiments invite the audience to play along — heckling and votes explicitly welcome.

Does anything from the talk stick — and does it inspire courage or fear?

Yes — courage, to be precise: vivid stories like the transport robot on wheels spark conversations that last well beyond the evening. Christoph Holz is deliberately optimistic — technology as a tool that people strengthen.
