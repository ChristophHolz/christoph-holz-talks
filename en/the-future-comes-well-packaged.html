The future comes well packaged

AI, robots, smart packaging — and the next packaging economy

All you see is packaging — the performance behind it stays invisible: it keeps food fresh, delivers fragile goods intact, carries the brand all the way to the shelf, and much more. Yet forms tie up valuable staff while raw materials grow scarce and young talent is missing. Digitalization as a cure-all? Of course not — companies already solve their own problems. But it pays to think the innovation potential of AI, robots, and sensors through to the end with forward thinker Christoph Holz — while also questioning the limits of technology. Those who understand how, win.

Three Thoughts from the Talk

We have long packaged not just products but promises: fresh, intact, on time. Smart packaging turns the promise into proof.

Raw materials grow scarce, so packaging grows smart: what needs less material needs more thinking — and of that we have plenty.

For decades we feared the robots would take our jobs. Demographics reverse the sentence: we are running out of people—and the machines are arriving just in time.

Through vivid metaphors and examples, your guests grasp what technology can do — and where its limits lie.

03

Reframing the skilled-labor and raw-materials question

Questions and Answers about the Talk

Does the talk address the specific needs of the audience?

Yes: examples and focal points are tailored to the industry in advance with the organizer — here, for instance, to regulation, skills shortages and robots.

Are participants met where they are?

Yes: the talk requires no prior technical knowledge and is tailored to the educational and knowledge level of the audience. Usually Holz joins in beforehand to get to know his listeners.

Is the talk interactive?

Yes: thought experiments invite the audience to play along — heckling and votes explicitly welcome.

Does anything from the talk stick — and does it inspire courage or fear?

Yes — and specifically courage: vivid stories like Nuro's reverse channel in the supply chain provide talking points that last well beyond the evening. Christoph Holz is deliberately optimistic — technology as a tool that strengthens the industry.

Christoph Holz's Expertise

Packaging

Christoph Holz knows packaging from the software all the way to the robot: for Dinkhauser Kartonagen he built the multi-portal for the brands PackIT, T4P and Aristos. At Mimic in Zurich he saw humanoid robots learning to pack and sort. And at the Nuro delivery van in Silicon Valley he discovered the supply chain's return channel: the packaging stays in the autonomous delivery van and simply rides back with it. In 2026 he got to know PulpStack: 3D printing for cardboard — sustainable, cost-efficient packaging in small batches. On stage he answers the question of why the packaging of the future doesn't end up in the trash, but simply rides back with it.

Manufacturing Industry

Even during his time at technical secondary school (HTL), Holz worked on the production floor at Kahlbacher, the world market leader for snow-clearing technology, and at Krupp in Essen. After his combined studies in computer science and mechanical engineering, Holz consulted for BMW engine manufacturing in Lohof. In his entrepreneurial career, Holz led projects for voestalpine Stahl AG, Thyssen and EGLO. Today Magna, Egger and Siemens bring him to the stage — where he answers the question of why the future of the factory is not a factory: the plant hall solves a problem that will soon no longer exist.

Logistics

Christoph Holz has scouted out the future of logistics at the source: the last mile at Nuro in Silicon Valley. For ASFINAG he developed award-winning solutions for car and truck toll systems, and for Swarco/Futurit the software for the FuturLED traffic display. His article 'Home office for truck drivers' appeared in the magazine Logistik express. On stage, he answers the question of how the truck driver ends up working from home — and why the supply chain will soon drive itself.

Industrial robots

Ever since he built his first industrial articulated-arm robot in 1986, industrial robotics has never let go of Christoph Holz: in 2014 he saw the welding robots at Tesla in Fremont, and in 2026 their next generation at Magna Steyr on the G-Class assembly line. At ABB he explored cobots. On stage, he answers the question of why robots are leaving their cages — and why an industrial robot needs hands with five fingers when three would do.

TV expert

Christoph Holz is a sought-after TV expert on digitalization, AI and the future — whether in the studio or from his private home film studio — appearing on RTL, ServusTV, ProSieben, Sat.1, n-tv, WELT, Deutsche Welle, Hamburg1, ORF and CNBC, among others.
